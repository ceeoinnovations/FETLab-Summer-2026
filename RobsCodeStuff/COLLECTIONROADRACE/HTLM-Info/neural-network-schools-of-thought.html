Which axis each tool sits on

  • Input — frame stacking / optical flow (what the network sees)
  • Output — action chunking (what shape the network's prediction takes)
  • Representation — self-supervised backbone + thin policy head (how the visual features are learned)
  • Supervision — multi-task / auxiliary loss (what extra signal trains the shared backbone)
  • Training loop — DAgger (how the training data itself is gathered)
  • Runtime — model-based control (what wraps the trained policy when it's actually driving)

Because each sits on its own axis, picking one doesn't use up your ability to pick another — you could stack all six into one policy: a frame-stacked, chunked-output, self-supervised-backbone, multi-task-trained, DAgger-corrected policy that's additionally wrapped in model-based re-planning at runtime. That's a mouthful, but it's a coherent, buildable system. The one caveat worth flagging: action chunking and model-based control both touch "how often do we commit to a plan," so combining them means deciding whether to replan every frame (which partly defeats chunking's point) or only every few frames — not a conflict, just a design choice the other four pairings don't force on you.

Why the top-level three-way split still holds

The families are separated by what supplies the training signal: behavior cloning needs a demonstrator, reinforcement learning needs a reward/fitness signal, self-supervised world modeling needs neither. The tools sitting under direct action imitation are a different kind of thing entirely — they're not alternative training paradigms, they're modifications along independent axes of one paradigm, which is exactly why nearly all of them compose rather than compete.
